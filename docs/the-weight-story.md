# The weight story: manuscript and storyboard

The `/science` page rewritten as one article. This file is the editorial source. If the page and this
file disagree, fix one of them on purpose.

**The question the page answers:** why does a weight that once felt heavy eventually become easier?

**What a reader should be able to explain at the end:**

1. How muscles produce force.
2. What actin and myosin do.
3. Why challenging training stimulates adaptation.
4. Why the same weight may become easier.
5. What progressive overload means.
6. Why repeating an identical workout can become a less effective challenge.
7. Why logging helps you train deliberately.

**Rules of the writing.** Real terms, introduced once, in plain sentences. One idea per paragraph. No
analogy that has to be un-learned later. Anything hypothetical says so. Growth and strength stay separate.
The optional detail lives in expandable notes. Claims link to numbered sources.

## Part 1: Manuscript

### Opening

**Remember when 10 kg felt heavy?**

The weight hasn’t changed since then. This is what did, from the curl down to the molecules, and what it
means for your next workout.

### 1. The weight hasn’t changed

You picked the dumbbell up, and by the seventh curl your arm was shaking. You finished the set, or nearly
did, and put it down faster than you meant to.

A few weeks later the same dumbbell came off the rack and it was fine. Nothing about it had changed. It’s
still 10 kg of iron, and gravity pulls on it exactly as hard.

So something changed in you. To find out what, start with the muscle doing the lifting.

The one on the front of your upper arm is the biceps brachii. It starts on your shoulder blade, crosses your
elbow and attaches to your forearm. When it shortens, your forearm comes up. Muscles can only pull, so every
weight you have ever lifted came down to something shortening.

How does a muscle shorten? You have to go inside to see it.

### 2. Inside your muscle

A muscle looks like one solid thing, but it’s built more like a rope: bundles, made of smaller bundles, made
of smaller ones still. Six steps take you from the biceps to the molecules that do the work.

- **Biceps brachii.** The whole muscle, wrapped in a sheath of connective tissue.
- **Fascicle.** Cut across the muscle and you find bundles called fascicles, each with its own wrapping.
  They’re the grain you can see in a piece of steak.
- **Muscle fiber.** Each fascicle holds muscle fibers. A fiber is a single cell, but an unusual one: up to a
  tenth of a millimetre wide, up to 30 cm long in the longest muscles, and with many nuclei instead of one.
- **Myofibril.** A fiber is packed with hundreds or thousands of thin rods called myofibrils, about 1.2
  micrometres across, running its full length.
- **Sarcomere.** Along each myofibril one unit repeats, end to end. That unit is the sarcomere, about 2
  micrometres long, with a wall called a Z-disc at each end. Around 5,000 fit in a single centimetre. It’s
  the smallest part of a muscle that can contract.
- **Actin and myosin.** Inside each sarcomere, two kinds of filament overlap. Thin filaments of actin are
  anchored to the Z-discs. Thick filaments of myosin sit in the middle, studded with tiny heads.

Those heads are where force begins.

### 3. How muscles actually pull

For a long time it wasn’t obvious how a muscle shortens. Then in 1954 two teams, working separately,
published the answer in the same issue of Nature: the filaments don’t shrink. They slide past each other.

Thick and thin filaments keep their length. What changes is how far they overlap. The thin filaments are
drawn in toward the middle of the sarcomere, the Z-discs come closer together, and the whole unit gets
shorter.

The sliding comes from a repeating cycle called the cross-bridge cycle, powered by a molecule called ATP.

1. **Attach.** A myosin head, already charged with energy, binds to actin. That link is the cross-bridge.
2. **Power stroke.** The head pivots toward the centre of the sarcomere and pulls the actin along with it, by
   a few billionths of a metre.
3. **Release.** A new ATP molecule binds to the head, and the head lets go. Without ATP it can’t let go,
   which is why muscles stiffen after death.
4. **Re-cock.** The head splits the ATP and uses that energy to swing back, ready to grab further along.

Heads don’t all pull at once. Some hold on while others reset, so the filament keeps moving instead of
slipping back.

One stroke is tiny, so how does it lift a dumbbell? Two kinds of adding up. Sarcomeres sit end to end, about
5,000 to the centimetre, and when each one shortens a little the whole fiber shortens a lot. That gives you
distance. Myofibrils and fibers sit side by side, and each one pulls at the same time. That gives you force.

*Note: What tells myosin to start?* A nerve signal makes the fiber release calcium. Calcium binds to a protein
called troponin, which moves another protein, tropomyosin, off the binding sites on actin. Now myosin can
attach. When the signal stops, calcium is pumped away and the sites are covered again.

That’s one rep. It doesn’t yet explain why the same dumbbell feels lighter next month. For that, we need what
happens after you put it down.

### 4. How training changes the muscle

When a load is hard for you, all that pulling puts the fiber under strain. The term for it is mechanical
tension. Your cells can sense it. The exact sensor is still being worked out, but tension is the leading
explanation for what starts muscle growth.

Sensing it switches on signalling inside the fiber, including a pathway called mTORC1, and the fiber starts
building protein faster. That lasts a day or so after a hard workout, then settles. Food supplies the
material, protein especially, and poor sleep can get in the way.

No single workout changes much that you could see. Repeated over weeks, this does two separate things.

**Your nervous system gets better at the lift.** Your spinal cord drives muscle through motor units: one nerve
cell and all the fibers it controls. With practice you recruit more of them, fire them faster, and coordinate
them better. You get stronger, without the muscle necessarily being any bigger.

**Your fibers get thicker.** This is hypertrophy. Fibers add contractile protein, so there’s more material
pulling side by side, and more force. Whether that means thicker myofibrils, more of them, or both is still
an open question.

The two overlap, and growth starts earlier than people assume, but strength and size aren’t the same thing.
In the first weeks, a lot of what you feel is your nervous system learning.

*Note: Does soreness mean it’s working?* Not reliably. Soreness mostly follows movements you aren’t used to.
Some muscle damage does happen, especially early on, and early protein building goes largely to repair. In one
study, growth only tracked protein synthesis once the damage had faded. Damage probably isn’t required for
growth at all.

### 5. 10 kg doesn’t feel the same anymore

Back to the dumbbell, in a made-up but typical example. Same person, same 10 kg, six weeks apart.

In week 1, eight curls is close to everything they have. In week 6, eight curls leaves plenty in the tank.

The load hasn’t moved. The share of their capacity it takes has. That share is what you feel, and it’s called
relative effort. A stronger, better-coordinated arm can lift the same 10 kg further from its limit.

How quickly this happens varies a lot from person to person. But it raises a question that didn’t exist in
week 1: now that eight curls is easier, what should change next?

### 6. Two ways to continue

Same person, next few weeks, two versions. Drag through them.

- **Repeats the workout:** 10 kg × 8, every time.
- **Records and progresses:** 10 × 8, 10 × 9, 10 × 10, 10 × 12, 12 × 8.

*Illustrative. Real progress is rarely this tidy.*

Neither person has wasted their time. Repeating a workout still trains you, and it can keep building muscle
for a while, especially for beginners. It just asks a little less of you each week, because you’re getting
further from your limit while it stays where it is.

The second person keeps the challenge matched to their capacity. That has a name: progressive overload. It
doesn’t mean adding weight every week. It means gradually asking more than your body is used to. One more rep
at the same weight counts, and so does an extra set. In one study of trained lifters, adding reps built muscle
about as well as adding weight.

A trial published in 2026 tested this directly. Untrained young women did the same triceps exercise for eight
weeks, with arms assigned to raise the load, keep it fixed, or not train.

| Arm | Triceps thickness |
| --- | --- |
| Load increased | +22.9% |
| Load kept the same | +11.6% |
| No training | +1.8% |

Both trained arms grew, and overload grew more. This was beginners, one exercise, two months. It doesn’t say
what happens over years, and it isn’t a forecast for you.

**The short version.** Myosin heads pull actin, powered by ATP. Sarcomeres shorten, fibers shorten, your arm
bends. A load that’s hard for you creates tension, and over weeks your nervous system improves and your fibers
thicken. So the same weight takes a smaller share of what you’ve got. It gets easier, and asks less of you.
Asking for a little more, in whatever way fits, is progressive overload.

### 7. This is why you remember your workouts

You might remember what you lifted today. But what about six weeks ago?

Writing a workout down doesn’t build any muscle, and plenty of people progress on feel or with a notebook.
What logging does is put last time in front of you before you start, so that one more rep is something you
decide rather than something you hope happened.

*The phone shows a real Stack log for Dumbbell Curl. Compare today with six weeks ago, log one more rep than
last time, and the workout lands on your stack.*

**Every workout stacks up.**
